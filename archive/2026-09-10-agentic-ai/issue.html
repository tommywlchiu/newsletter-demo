42% of organisations have agents. 15% have scaled them. And the 88% failure stat everyone quotes has no source.
ISSUE 001  ·  10 SEPTEMBER 2026

Agentic AI is genuinely shipping — but as bounded systems inside customer service, software delivery and workflow automation, not as autonomous digital employees. The interesting question is no longer whether agents work in a demo. It's what happens on step 17.
Every model collapsed within 16 steps
Benchmark scores do not survive contact with long tasks.
A study submitted on 31 August tested nine models — six open ones ranging from 1.2 billion to 671 billion parameters, plus three deployed proprietary systems — across 10,664 trajectories. On the agentic tool-use task, every single model fell from near-perfect success to near zero within 16 steps.
That's the finding worth sitting with. Not "some models degrade at length" — all of them did, inside a horizon shorter than most real workflows. Errors compound, and a chain of dependent actions is exactly where compounding bites.
Fair caveat: this is a preprint, not an audited field study. But it explains something practitioners keep reporting — a system that aces a benchmark still falls over on a Tuesday. A second preprint makes the operational version of the same point: two clinical-audit systems with autonomous accuracy of 72.8% and 72.5% — a gap of 0.3 points — needed 39.2% versus 29.6% human review to hit the same reliability target. Near-identical scores, materially different running costs.
Nine models. Not one of them held past step 16.
Having agents and scaling agents are different things
The gap between the two is where most of the disappointment lives.
Deloitte surveyed 501 U.S. senior managers and executives between April and June. 42% had tested or deployed AI agents. Only 15% had scaled orchestrated, multi-agent adoption. Just 5% said their business processes were highly prepared for agents — and 70% said they did not feel able to trust and govern them.
McKinsey's numbers read more optimistically: 40% of respondents at organisations above $1 billion in revenue said they were scaling agents in at least one function, against 22% at smaller ones. Those aren't contradictory figures, they're different questions — McKinsey counts scaling in a single function and doesn't require multi-agent orchestration. Worth knowing which definition a headline is leaning on.
As for why the funnel narrows: the most credible failure mode isn't dramatic hallucination, it's context. In a July survey of 101 enterprises, 68% traced at least one confident-but-wrong agent answer to missing or inconsistent business context in the prior six months, and 37% said it kept happening. Stale data, inconsistent metric definitions, incomplete retrieval. The model isn't lying to you — it's being handed the wrong version of your business and reasoning impeccably from it.
Same survey, same respondents — so the drop-off is real, not an artefact of comparing studies.
Three numbers that keep getting repeated
All three fall apart when you look for the primary source.
The claim that 88% of agent pilots never reach production is everywhere. Searching for the study behind it turns up secondary blogs citing each other, not an identifiable primary publication. Treat it as folklore until someone produces the source.
Gartner's "more than 40%" is real but routinely misread. It is a forecast for agentic-AI projects that will be cancelled by the end of 2027 — not an observed failure rate that has already happened.
The subtlest one: organisations that built a semantic or context layer reported more recurring failures than those without. That reads like an indictment until you notice the direction of causation — a context layer is what lets you detect and attribute failures you were previously shipping blind. The source warns about this explicitly. Better instrumentation, not worse agents.
Check the primary source before quoting any of these.
Four weeks of plumbing, not demos
What actually shipped recently is deployment control, not new capability.
GitLab put agentic workloads inside its single-tenant Dedicated environment on 20 August, aimed at regulated customers. AWS made its Agent Registry generally available across five regions — governed discovery of agents, tools, skills and MCP servers. OpenAI's Agents API entered public beta today.
Salesforce, meanwhile, offered rare production telemetry: 7.0 billion agentic work units delivered to date, 3.2 billion in fiscal Q2 alone, up 97% quarter over quarter. That's vendor-reported and specific to one platform — it isn't a market-wide deployment rate, and it doesn't establish that every task was autonomous or profitable. But it's a lot more concrete than a pilot count.
The through-line: identity, tenancy, tool permissions, registries. Nobody is shipping a smarter agent this month. They're shipping the machinery for letting one near your production systems without losing track of what it did.
The pattern is governance infrastructure, not model capability.
